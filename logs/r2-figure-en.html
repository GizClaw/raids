第 4 轮

用户原话：
“Master Li, when people claim Suiye as your birthplace, what kind of claim is it? Is it based on evidence, such as family records or documents; on someone’s direct observation; or on a story repeated until it became tradition? And I would ask likewise of the tale that, as a truant boy, you met an old woman grinding an iron pestle into a needle. Did you truly meet her and live that moment, or was that story later woven around your name? Tell me which memories are yours, and which belong to the world.”

主持完整回复：
The traveler has asked the right question: not where Li Bai was born, but what kind of claim each story rests on. The answer divides cleanly into two layers — one rooted in near-contemporary documents, the other a tale woven by later generations.

On the birthplace: the Suiye claim is not a family record, nor a direct observation by anyone who was present at the birth. It is an inference drawn from the earliest surviving Tang sources. Li Yangbing — Li Bai's cousin-uncle, the magistrate of Dangtu who received the dying poet's manuscripts in 762 and wrote the *Preface to the Thatched Cottage Collection* — recorded that the family "fled and returned to Shu" in the Shenlong era (705 CE). Fan Chuanzheng's later memorial stele adds that a branch of the clan had been "exiled to Suiye" in the Sui dynasty. Since Li Bai's own *Self-Recommendation to Commissioner Song* (757) gives his age as fifty-seven, scholars calculate a birth year of 701 — four years before the family's recorded return to Shu. That arithmetic is what places his birth in the Western Regions, near modern Tokmok in Kyrgyzstan. No document says outright "Li Bai was born at Suiye"; the conclusion is reconstructed from dates and migration records. The alternative — birth in Shu itself — rests on the same sources read differently, and on Li Bai's own habit of calling himself a man of Shu. Both accounts are ancient; neither is eyewitness.

On the iron pestle: this is not Li Bai's memory at all. The story of the truant boy who met an old woman grinding an iron rod into a needle, and was shamed into diligence, does not appear in any Tang source. It surfaces for the first time in the *Fang Yu Sheng Lan* (方輿勝覽), a geographical compendium compiled by the Southern Song scholar Zhu Mu — roughly four hundred years after Li Bai's death. No Tang writer, not Li Yangbing, not Wei Hao, not Fan Chuanzheng, mentions such an encounter. It belongs to the world, not to the poet: a parable about perseverance that attached itself to his name because his legend already demanded a story of youthful transformation.

So the line is drawn plainly. The birthplace dispute is a genuine historical puzzle built from documents that Li Bai himself helped hand down. The grinding-pestle tale is a later invention — a moral fable the world chose to tell about him, not a moment he ever lived.
