第 3 轮

用户原话：
“Master Li, if I may ask something that has long puzzled me—do the accounts of where you were born differ? I have heard more than one place named, and I would know the truth from your own lips.”

主持完整回复：
The question concerns Li Bai's birthplace, and the surviving accounts do not give a single settled answer. According to the present record, there are two main traditions: one places his birth in Suiye, in the Western Regions, near present-day Tokmok in Kyrgyzstan, with the family moving to Changlong County, Mianzhou—present-day Jiangyou in Sichuan—when he was about five; the other places his birth directly in Shu. Since the sources themselves are divided, the later tradition cannot be reduced to one certain place from the materials now available.

The same record also fixes the broad outline of his early life: he was born around 701, grew up in Shu during his youth, left Shu around 725, and in 742 was summoned to Chang'an as a Hanlin scholar. Thus, whether he was born in Suiye or in Shu, his formative years were in Sichuan, and the later stories of his youth are tied to that region.

The earlier question about his time in Jinling after his money ran out belongs to the same period of wandering. The present materials do not name a friend who rescued him in Jinling at that moment, so the account should not invent one. What can be said is that he was traveling alone and living by his wits, his poetry, and whatever support he could find on the road.

第 4 轮

用户原话：
“Master Li, when people claim you were born in Suiye, do they speak from evidence, from someone’s observed record, or from a story handed down afterward? And tell me this too: the tale of the old woman grinding an iron pestle into a needle—was that something you truly witnessed in your youth, or is it a tradition that later attached itself to your name?”

主持完整回复：
The question about Suiye is answered by the nature of the surviving sources, not by a single eyewitness record. The earliest accounts are Li Yangbing's *Preface to the Cottage Collection* and Fan Chuanzheng's tomb inscription. Li Yangbing was Li Bai's clansman and the magistrate of Dangtu; he wrote the preface at Li Bai's own request near the end of the poet's life, so the family material likely came from Li Bai's oral account. Fan Chuanzheng later visited Dangtu and saw a family memorandum preserved by Li Bai's son, Boqin. Both sources say the family was exiled to the western frontier and later returned to Shu. They do not, however, provide a direct, contemporary birth certificate naming Suiye.

The Suiye conclusion is therefore an inference drawn from chronology rather than an observed record. Li Bai's own *Self-Recommendation Table for Chancellor Song*, written in 757, says he was then fifty-seven; counting back gives a birth year of 701. The family's return to Shu is placed around 705, when Li Bai would have been about five. Scholars who favor Suiye argue that this gap means he must have been born in the western frontier before the family moved. Scholars who favor Shu point to the phrase "returned and fled to Shu, then pointed to the plum tree and gave birth to Boyang" in Li Yangbing's preface, reading it as meaning Li Bai was born after the family reached Sichuan. The sources themselves are ambiguous, and the debate has continued for centuries.

The tale of the old woman grinding an iron pestle into a needle is a later tradition, not a Tang-documented event. Its earliest known appearance is in Zhu Mu's *Fang Yü Sheng Lan*, a Southern Song geographical work compiled in the 13th century, roughly four hundred years after Li Bai's death. The text says: "At the stream for grinding needles, under Mount Xiang'e, it is traditionally said that Li Taibai was reading in the mountains; before finishing, he abandoned his studies and left. Crossing the stream, he met an old woman grinding an iron pestle. He asked her what she was doing. She said, 'I want to make a needle.' Li Bai was moved by her resolve and returned to complete his studies."

No Tang source records this encounter. The story is best understood as a moral legend attached to Li Bai's name, illustrating perseverance rather than a verified episode of his youth. It should not be presented as something he personally witnessed.
